# **September 12, 2026 — Psychology**

## **The Idea**

The Hawthorne effect is a psychological phenomenon where individuals modify or improve their behavior and productivity simply because they know they are being observed. In the original workplace lighting experiments, researchers discovered that worker output increased not because of physical changes in illumination, but because employees felt noticed and valued by investigators. This demonstrated that social attention and the perception of being monitored significantly alter human performance independent of material variables.

## **Analogy**

It is like driving strictly within the legal speed limit and keeping both hands on the wheel the moment you notice a marked police cruiser driving behind you.

## **Nested Terms**

Demand characteristics refers to subtle experimental cues that reveal what behavior the researcher expects, unconsciously leading participants to conform to those expectations.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because organizations typically assume physical perks, equipment upgrades, or environmental tweaks drive productivity, whereas social awareness often produces the initial surge in output. Modern researchers heavily debate the original studies, noting that productivity gains were likely driven by regular performance feedback, novelty, and learning curves rather than mere passive observation.

## **Source**

Based on the 1924–1932 Western Electric Hawthorne Works studies led by Elton Mayo, and popularized by sociologist Henry A. Landsberger in 1958\. For further reading, see the [Simply Psychology entry on the Hawthorne Effect](https://www.simplypsychology.org/hawthorne-effect.html).

# **September 13, 2026 — Thought Experiment**

## **The Idea**

Buridan's Ass is a philosophical thought experiment illustrating decision paralysis in the face of equally balanced alternatives. Imagine a hungry donkey placed precisely midway between two identical, equally appetizing piles of hay. Because the animal has completely equal reason to turn toward either option and lacks any rational justification to prefer one over the other, it remains frozen in indecision and starves to death. The puzzle demonstrates how purely deterministic rational choice can paradoxically lead to catastrophic outcomes without a mechanism for breaking symmetry.

## **Analogy**

It is like standing paralyzed in a grocery aisle staring at two identical cans of soup with the exact same price and brand, wasting valuable time unable to decide which one to grab.

## **Nested Terms**

Symmetry breaking refers to a process where a small random perturbation or arbitrary factor disrupts an evenly balanced state, allowing a definitive choice or transition to occur.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because survival instincts should compel an organism to simply pick at random, yet strict rational determinism demands a sufficient reason for every action. Philosophers and decision theorists heavily debate whether absolute ties ever exist in real life, with thinkers like Baruch Spinoza and modern cognitive scientists arguing that random neural noise and subtle micro-preferences always intervene to resolve parity.

## **Source**

Named after 14th-century French philosopher Jean Buridan, though traced to earlier discussions by Aristotle. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on John Buridan](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/buridan/).

# **September 14, 2026 — Philosophy**

## **The Idea**

Bad faith (mauvaise foi) is an existentialist concept describing a form of self-deception where individuals pretend they lack freedom and choice to escape the anxiety of personal responsibility. Jean-Paul Sartre argued that human beings are fundamentally free, continuously defining who they are through their ongoing choices and actions. When someone hides behind social roles, societal expectations, or perceived destiny by claiming "I had no choice," they reduce themselves from an active conscious agent into a passive object.

## **Analogy**

It is like a cafe waiter who exaggerates his robotic movements and formal posture, turning himself into a theatrical prop to pretend that being a waiter is his unalterable essence rather than a choice he renews every morning.

## **Nested Terms**

Facticity refers to the unchangeable concrete facts of our existence, such as our birthplace, past history, and physical limitations, that form the context within which our choices occur.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because people often feel genuinely trapped by external obligations and economic demands, whereas Sartre insists that accepting any external constraint as insurmountable is an active choice. Critics and modern ethicists debate Sartre's radical view of absolute freedom, arguing that severe socio-economic deprivation, trauma, and systemic oppression constrain genuine life options far more than his early philosophy acknowledged.

## **Source**

Formulated by French philosopher Jean-Paul Sartre in his 1943 work Being and Nothingness. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Jean-Paul Sartre](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sartre/).

# **September 15, 2026 — Psychology**

## **The Idea**

The Stroop effect is a cognitive phenomenon demonstrating delayed reaction times and mental interference when processing conflicting sensory information. When asked to state the physical ink color of a word whose text spells out a different color (such as the word "GREEN" printed in red ink), individuals respond significantly slower and make more mistakes. This occurs because reading written words is an automatic, deeply conditioned brain process that directly collides with the conscious, controlled effort of identifying visual colors.

## **Analogy**

It is like trying to pat your head while rubbing your stomach in circles—two conflicting neural programs competing for the same mental processing bandwidth at the exact same moment.

## **Nested Terms**

Automaticity refers to the cognitive ability of the brain to execute complex tasks rapidly and unconsciously with minimal mental effort or deliberate attention.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because identifying a clear visual color seems trivial, yet deeply ingrained literacy reflexes effortlessly hijack conscious attention. Cognitive psychologists continue to debate whether the interference happens during early perceptual processing (recognizing the conflicting stimulus) or during late response selection (verbally articulating the answer).

## **Source**

First experimentally demonstrated by American psychologist John Ridley Stroop in his 1935 study Studies of interference in serial verbal reactions. For further reading, see the [Simply Psychology entry on the Stroop Effect](https://www.simplypsychology.org/stroop-effect.html).

# **September 16, 2026 — Philosophy**

## **The Idea**

The Absurd is a philosophical concept describing the fundamental conflict between the human longing for inherent meaning, purpose, and clarity, and the cold, silent indifference of the universe. Albert Camus argued that when we realize life lacks an ultimate cosmic design, we should neither retreat into false spiritual illusions nor surrender to despair. Instead, we should embrace the absurdity of existence through conscious defiance, choosing to live passionately, authentically, and fully in the present moment.

## **Analogy**

It is like continuing to build an elaborate, beautiful sandcastle right by the ocean tide—knowing the waves will inevitably wash it away, yet finding joy and purpose in the act of building itself.

## **Nested Terms**

Philosophical suicide refers to the act of evading the tension of the absurd by adopting dogmatic beliefs or religious doctrines that promise an unproven transcendent meaning.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because discovering that life has no inherent purpose is typically assumed to cause depression and nihilism, whereas Camus asserts that confronting absurdity is the ultimate prerequisite for genuine freedom and happiness. Critics and existentialist contemporaries, including Jean-Paul Sartre, debated Camus's stance, arguing that humans can and must actively construct political and moral projects rather than merely rebelling against an indifferent cosmos.

## **Source**

Formulated by French-Algerian philosopher Albert Camus in his 1942 essay The Myth of Sisyphus. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Albert Camus](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/camus/).

# **September 17, 2026 — Psychology**

## **The Idea**

Inattentional blindness (or perceptual blindness) is a cognitive phenomenon where an individual fails to perceive an unexpected stimulus that is in plain sight because their attention is focused intensely on another task. Because human cognitive capacity and visual attention are strictly finite, the brain actively filters out surrounding visual information deemed irrelevant to prevent sensory overload. Consequently, even dramatic or obvious objects completely escape conscious awareness if attention is engaged elsewhere.

## **Analogy**

It is like watching a video of basketball players and counting how many passes they make, completely failing to notice a person in a full gorilla suit walking into the center of the screen and beating their chest.

## **Nested Terms**

Attentional capacity refers to the finite cognitive resources available in the brain for processing sensory information and executing mental tasks at any given moment.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because people intuitively assume that their eyes function like video cameras capturing everything within their visual field, making it shocking to learn that seeing requires focused attention. Cognitive psychologists debate the exact mechanics of the filter, investigating whether unattended stimuli are registered subconsciously before being discarded, or blocked entirely from early sensory processing.

## **Source**

First experimentally named by psychologists Arien Mack and Irvin Rock (1992) and famously demonstrated by Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris in their 1999 "Invisible Gorilla" study. For further reading, see the [Simply Psychology entry on Inattentional Blindness](https://www.simplypsychology.org/inattentional-blindness.html).

# **September 18, 2026 — Thought Experiment**

## **The Idea**

The Tragedy of the Commons is a classic thought experiment illustrating how rational individuals, acting independently in their private self-interest, can ultimately deplete and ruin a shared, finite resource. Imagine an open communal pasture where local farmers graze their cattle. Because each farmer receives the full economic benefit of adding an extra animal while the environmental cost of overgrazing is distributed equally among everyone, all farmers are incentivized to continually expand their herds until the pasture collapses into barren ruin. The scenario demonstrates why unmanaged open-access resources suffer systemic degradation without collective rules or institutional governance.

## **Analogy**

It is like roommates sharing a communal refrigerator where everyone takes extra milk or leaves dirty dishes assuming someone else will clean up, eventually rendering the shared kitchen unusable for everyone.

## **Nested Terms**

Negative externality refers to an unintended harmful cost or consequence of an individual's action that is imposed onto other people or the broader public without compensation.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because classic economic intuition suggests that individuals pursuing private self-interest naturally optimize collective prosperity, whereas the commons proves that unrestricted individual rationality can produce aggregate disaster. Nobel laureate Elinor Ostrom famously contested Hardin's fatalistic conclusion, proving through extensive empirical field research that communities worldwide frequently avoid collapse by creating decentralized, self-governing institutions and cooperative norms without needing state coercion or full privatization.

## **Source**

Formulated by ecologist Garrett Hardin in his 1968 paper The Tragedy of the Commons. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on The Free Rider Problem](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/free-rider/).

# **September 19, 2026 — Philosophy**

## **The Idea**

Epicurus argued that the fear of death is irrational because death involves the complete deprivation of consciousness and sensory experience. If all good and evil consist in conscious sensation (pleasure and pain), and death is the total absence of sensation, then being dead cannot actually harm the person who dies. As Epicurus famously summarized: "When we are, death is not; and when death exists, we are not." Because a person never exists simultaneously with their own non-existence, death cannot be experienced as painful or bad.

## **Analogy**

It is like being needlessly anxious about what you were missing during the billions of years before you were born—an absence of experience that caused you zero discomfort.

## **Nested Terms**

Hedonism is the ethical and philosophical theory that pleasure (or the absence of pain) is the only intrinsic good, and pain is the only intrinsic evil.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because human beings have a deep-seated evolutionary fear of mortality, instinctively viewing death as the ultimate tragedy. Contemporary philosophers heavily contest Epicurus's conclusion through the "deprivation account," arguing that death is indeed an evil not because it inflicts positive suffering, but because it permanently deprives an individual of the future goods, achievements, and happiness they would have otherwise enjoyed.

## **Source**

Formulated by ancient Greek philosopher Epicurus in his Letter to Menoeceus (c. 300 BC) and expanded by Roman poet Lucretius. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Death](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/death/).

# **September 20, 2026 — Psychology**

## **The Idea**

The false consensus effect is a cognitive bias where individuals overestimate the degree to which their personal beliefs, habits, and behavioral choices are shared by the general public. People instinctively project their own subjective worldview onto others, assuming that their preferences are normal, standard, and widely endorsed. Consequently, when encountering individuals who hold conflicting viewpoints, they tend to view those alternative perspectives as uncommon, deviant, or irrational.

## **Analogy**

It is like someone who enjoys putting hot sauce on their eggs every morning assuming that nearly all breakfast diners do the same, and being genuinely astonished when others find it unusual.

## **Nested Terms**

Social projection refers to the psychological tendency to estimate other people's traits, opinions, and preferences by using one's own self-knowledge as a baseline anchor.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because human beings believe their estimates of societal opinions are grounded in broad social observation, whereas in reality they rely on biased self-sampling and like-minded social circles. Social psychologists debate whether the effect stems primarily from cognitive availability (our own thoughts are most accessible) or motivational needs to validate our self-esteem and feel part of a reassuring majority.

## **Source**

Formulated by social psychologist Lee Ross and colleagues in their 1977 landmark study The "False Consensus Effect": An Egocentric Bias in Social Perception and Attribution Processes. For further reading, see the [Simply Psychology entry on the False Consensus Effect](https://www.simplypsychology.org/false-consensus-effect.html).

# **September 21, 2026 — Thought Experiment**

## **The Idea**

The Beetle in the Box is a philosophical thought experiment exploring whether private, internal mental sensations can establish the meaning of our words. Imagine a group of people where each person possesses a small box containing something they call a "beetle," but no one is ever allowed to look into anyone else"s box. Each person understands what a beetle is solely by inspecting their own box. Ludwig Wittgenstein argued that if the word "beetle" functions meaningfully in their shared language, it cannot refer to the actual private object inside, because that object could be completely different for everyone, constantly changing, or even completely empty.

## **Analogy**

It is like two computer servers exchanging packets labeled "Status\_OK"—the actual microscopic voltage states inside each machine"s physical hardware are completely hidden and irrelevant, provided the shared communication protocol operates consistently.

## **Nested Terms**

Private language argument refers to the philosophical thesis that a language understandable by only a single individual, referring exclusively to inaccessible inner sensations, is logically impossible because it lacks public standards of correctness.

## **Why It"s Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because human beings instinctively believe words for subjective feelings (like "pain" or "joy") directly name private, internal mental objects that only they can truly perceive. Dualists and philosophers of mind heavily contest Wittgenstein"s conclusion, arguing that conscious experiences and qualia possess intrinsic experiential reality that cannot be reduced to public linguistic conventions or functional behavior.

## **Source**

Formulated by philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein in §293 of his 1953 work Philosophical Investigations. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Private Language](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/private-language/).

# **September 22, 2026 — Philosophy**

## **The Idea**

The Panopticon is an architectural and philosophical model of institutional surveillance designed by social reformer Jeremy Bentham. The structure features a circular building of individual cells arranged around a central watchtower, designed so that a single supervisor can observe any inmate, while inmates can never see into the tower. Because occupants know they could be monitored at any given second, they are compelled to assume they are under constant surveillance, internalizing self-policing discipline. Bentham argued that this psychological mechanism maximizes institutional order, deterrence, and behavioral reform with minimal enforcement resources.

## **Analogy**

It is like driving down a highway with automated speed cameras—because you cannot tell which camera housings contain active radar and which are empty, you consistently regulate your speed past every single one.

## **Nested Terms**

Internalized discipline refers to the psychological process where individuals regulate their own behavior according to external rules even without an active enforcer present, because they anticipate potential monitoring.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because traditional control relies on heavy physical chains, thick walls, and constant corporal punishment, whereas the Panopticon achieves behavioral compliance through psychological uncertainty and sightlines alone. French philosopher Michel Foucault famously critiqued Bentham's model in Discipline and Punish, arguing that the Panopticon serves as a disturbing blueprint for modern surveillance societies, where omnipresent observation subjugates human autonomy and enforces passive conformity.

## **Source**

## **The Idea**

Hindsight bias (often called the "I-knew-it-all-along" effect) is a cognitive bias where individuals perceive past events as having been far more predictable than they actually were before they occurred. Once an outcome is known, the human brain automatically reconstructs its memory of the event, making the final result feel inevitable and obvious in retrospect. This mental distortion blurs our memory of earlier uncertainty, falsely convincing us that we foresaw the outcome all along.

## **Analogy**

It is like watching an unpredictable sports match with a surprising upset finish, and immediately claiming afterward that you could tell from the very first minute that the underdog was going to win.

## **Nested Terms**

Creeping determinism refers to the cognitive tendency to view an outcome, once it has occurred, as having been relatively inevitable and foreseeable from the start.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because people genuinely believe their memories accurately record their prior expectations, whereas the brain actively overwrites past uncertainty with present knowledge. Decision theorists and psychologists debate whether hindsight bias is purely a cognitive malfunction or an adaptive learning mechanism that helps the brain simplify chaotic events into coherent causal narratives, despite the risk of breeding unwarranted overconfidence in future forecasting.

## **Source**

First experimentally documented by psychologist Baruch Fischhoff in his 1975 study Hindsight ≠ foresight: The effect of outcome knowledge on judgment under uncertainty. For further reading, see the [Simply Psychology entry on Cognitive Bias](https://www.simplypsychology.org/cognitive-bias.html).

# **September 24, 2026 — Thought Experiment**

## **The Idea**

The Ship of Neurath (or Neurath's Boat) is an epistemological thought experiment that challenges the belief that human knowledge requires a bedrock, indubitable foundation. Otto Neurath famously proposed that we are like sailors on the open ocean who must rebuild every plank of our rotting ship while keeping it afloat, without ever being able to dock in dry dock to reconstruct it from scratch. We can examine, repair, or replace any individual belief or hypothesis, but only by temporarily standing on the remaining surrounding planks. Consequently, human science and knowledge advance through continuous, piecemeal revision and coherence rather than Cartesian certainty.

## **Analogy**

It is like refactoring and upgrading a massive software operating system while it runs live in production, swapping out individual legacy modules one by one without ever shutting down the server.

## **Nested Terms**

Coherentism is an epistemological theory holding that a belief is justified not by resting on foundational axioms, but by how well it coheres with a mutually supportive web of other beliefs.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because classic Cartesian intuition insists that knowledge must be grounded on immovable bedrock to be valid, whereas Neurath demonstrates that all human understanding is inherently provisional and dynamic. Foundationalist philosophers contest coherentism through the "isolation objection," arguing that an entirely self-consistent network of beliefs could easily remain coherent while drifting completely detached from external empirical reality.

## **Source**

Formulated by Austrian philosopher Otto Neurath in his 1932 paper Protocol Sentences and later popularized by W.V.O. Quine in Word and Object. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Otto Neurath](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/neurath/).

# **September 25, 2026 — Philosophy**

## **The Idea**

Qualitative hedonism is John Stuart Mill's refinement of utilitarianism, asserting that pleasures differ not only in quantity (duration and intensity) but also in intrinsic quality. While Jeremy Bentham famously argued that "push-pin is as good as poetry" if it creates equal pleasure, Mill countered that intellectual, artistic, and moral pleasures are qualitatively superior to base physical appetites. As Mill asserted, "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." Therefore, genuine human flourishing requires cultivating higher cognitive and cultural capacities rather than chasing mindless sensory gratification.

## **Analogy**

It is like choosing to spend an evening learning a challenging musical instrument rather than endlessly scrolling junk videos on social media—the initial effort might cause mild frustration, but the resulting fulfillment possesses a far richer quality.

## **Nested Terms**

Competent judges test refers to Mill's criterion that if individuals who have experienced both types of pleasure consistently prefer one over the other, that chosen pleasure is objectively of higher quality.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because classic hedonism equates all pleasure strictly to raw sensation, making it controversial to introduce subjective hierarchies of "better" or "worse" happiness. Critics heavily debate Mill's formulation, arguing that ranking pleasures introduces non-hedonistic values (like intellectual elitism or personal dignity) that contradict the foundational premise that pleasure alone is the ultimate good.

## **Source**

Formulated by philosopher John Stuart Mill in chapter 2 of his 1861 treatise Utilitarianism. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Mill's Moral and Political Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mill-moral-political/).

# **September 26, 2026 — Psychology**

## **The Idea**

The self-serving bias is a cognitive distortion where individuals attribute their successes to internal, personal factors (such as talent, skill, or effort), while blaming their failures on external, situational factors (such as bad luck, unfair rules, or difficult colleagues). This psychological mechanism acts as a protective shield for self-esteem, allowing people to take credit for positive outcomes while deflecting personal accountability for negative results. While it preserves confidence, it frequently distorts objective self-evaluation and prevents constructive learning from mistakes.

## **Analogy**

It is like an athlete who proudly credits their intense training and discipline after winning a race, but angrily blames the slippery track and poor officiating after losing the next one.

## **Nested Terms**

Locus of control refers to an individual's psychological belief regarding whether the outcomes of their life are determined primarily by internal personal actions or external environmental forces.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because people believe their self-evaluations are honest and balanced, yet subconscious ego-defense mechanisms systematically slant causal attribution. Cross-cultural psychologists debate its universality, showing that while self-serving bias is widespread in individualistic societies, people in collectivistic cultures frequently exhibit a "modesty bias," attributing successes to collective support and internalizing failures as personal deficits to be corrected.

## **Source**

Formulated in attribution theory by psychologist Dale Miller and Michael Ross in their 1975 paper Self-serving biases in the attribution of causality: Fact or fiction?. For further reading, see the [Simply Psychology entry on Self-Serving Bias](https://www.simplypsychology.org/self-serving-bias.html).

# **September 27, 2026 — Philosophy**

## **The Idea**

The Problem of Evil is a foundational philosophical dilemma examining the contradiction between the existence of profound suffering and the concept of an all-powerful, all-knowing, and wholly benevolent God. If a deity possesses infinite power, it has the capacity to abolish suffering; if it possesses infinite knowledge, it knows suffering exists; and if it is perfectly good, it would desire to eliminate evil. Because widespread, horrific suffering undeniably exists in the world, philosophers argue that at least one of these traditional theological premises must logically be false.

## **Analogy**

It is like an expert lifeguard standing on a watchtower who sees a swimmer struggling, has a motorized rescue boat ready, yet chooses to do nothing while the person drowns—one would conclude the lifeguard is either helpless, unaware, or malicious.

## **Nested Terms**

Theodicy refers to a philosophical defense or theological attempt to vindicate divine justice and benevolence despite the undeniable existence of evil and suffering.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because religious intuition assumes that a benevolent creator created the cosmos for moral good, yet gratuitous natural disasters and diseases appear completely arbitrary and cruel. Theologians and religious philosophers heavily contest the argument through the "Free Will Defense" and soul-making theodicies, arguing that genuine human moral agency and spiritual maturation logically necessitate the possibility of suffering.

## **Source**

Famously articulated in Greek philosophy by Epicurus, formulated in modern philosophy by David Hume in his 1779 Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, and formalized as the logical problem of evil by J.L. Mackie (1955). For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on The Problem of Evil](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/evil/).

# **September 28, 2026 — Thought Experiment**

## **The Idea**

The Survival Lottery is a thought experiment in bioethics testing the limits of raw utilitarian calculation versus individual rights. Imagine that organ transplant technology is perfected, allowing doctors to reliably save two or more dying patients by harvesting organs from one healthy person. To maximize the total number of surviving lives, philosopher John Harris proposed an institutional lottery where a central computer randomly selects a healthy citizen whenever organ shortages occur, sacrificing one person so their organs save multiple others. The thought experiment forces us to consider whether a society purely dedicated to maximizing aggregate survival numbers could ever ethically justify killing innocent individuals for the greater good.

## **Analogy**

It is like an emergency triage coordinator deciding to euthanize a healthy patient waiting in the hospital lounge to harvest their organs and rescue three accident victims in intensive care.

## **Nested Terms**

Rule utilitarianism is the ethical framework asserting that actions are morally right if they conform to general social rules that, when universally followed, maximize overall net well-being.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because simple arithmetic suggests that saving two lives at the cost of one maximizes survival, yet moral intuition firmly rejects compulsory human sacrifice. Deontologists and rule utilitarians argue against such a lottery, pointing out that institutionalizing involuntary harvesting would destroy public trust in healthcare and create pervasive societal terror, producing secondary harms that overwhelmingly destroy collective utility.

## **Source**

Formulated by bioethicist John Harris in his 1975 paper The Survival Lottery. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Consequentialism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consequentialism/).

# **September 29, 2026 — Psychology**

## **The Idea**

The bystander effect is a social psychological phenomenon where individuals are less likely to offer help to a victim when other people are present. As the number of onlookers increases, each person's perceived personal responsibility diminishes, assuming someone else will intervene. Consequently, emergencies occurring in crowded settings often result in delayed assistance compared to situations with a lone witness.

## **Analogy**

It is like noticing an overflowing trash bin in a busy office kitchen and walking past it, assuming one of your dozens of coworkers will surely empty it.

## **Nested Terms**

Diffusion of responsibility refers to the psychological tendency for individuals in a group to feel less accountable for taking action because the obligation is shared among all observers.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because common sense suggests that a larger crowd provides a safer safety net with more potential helpers. However, social psychologists heavily debate the universality of the effect; recent global analyses of real-world surveillance footage demonstrate that bystanders actually intervene in the vast majority of public conflicts, showing that severe physical danger often overrides hesitation.

## **Source**

First experimentally demonstrated by social psychologists John Darley and Bibb Latané in their 1968 study *Bystander intervention in emergencies: Diffusion of responsibility*. For further reading, see the [Simply Psychology entry on the Bystander Effect](https://www.simplypsychology.org/bystander-effect.html).

# **September 30, 2026 — Philosophy**

## **The Idea**

The Veil of Ignorance is a thought experiment in political philosophy designed to discover the fundamental principles of a fair and just society. John Rawls proposed that when establishing basic social institutions, decision-makers must imagine themselves positioned behind a veil that conceals their identity, including their wealth, race, gender, and natural abilities. Because no participant knows whether they will enter society as a billionaire or an impoverished minority, rational self-interest compels everyone to create rules that protect the least advantaged and maximize fairness for all.

## **Analogy**

It is like having one person slice a cake knowing that someone else gets to pick the first piece, strongly motivating the slicer to cut every piece equally.

## **Nested Terms**

The original position refers to the hypothetical starting state in social contract theory where rational individuals deliberate behind the veil of ignorance to determine fair societal principles.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because real-world political bargaining almost always relies on leveraging existing power, wealth, and self-interest, whereas Rawls demonstrates that genuine justice requires blinding ourselves to our personal advantages. Libertarian philosophers like Robert Nozick heavily contested this model, arguing that enforcing Rawlsian redistribution violates individual property rights and self-ownership.

## **Source**

Formulated by American philosopher John Rawls in his 1971 treatise A Theory of Justice. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on John Rawls](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rawls/).

# **October 3, 2026 — Thought Experiment**

## **The Idea**

The Chinese Room is a classic thought experiment challenging the claim that computers can possess genuine understanding or a conscious mind. Imagine an English speaker locked in a room with a rulebook that instructs how to match incoming Chinese characters with corresponding response symbols. To observers outside sliding questions under the door, the room's fluent replies make it appear fully literate. However, because the person inside merely shuffles symbols without understanding their meaning, John Searle argues that running a computer program never produces true mental comprehension.

## **Analogy**

It is like using a lookup sheet to substitute encrypted codes into other symbols without knowing what message is actually being conveyed.

## **Nested Terms**

Syntax refers to the formal rules and arrangements of symbols, whereas semantics refers to the actual meaning and mental content behind those symbols.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because the room passes the Turing test effortlessly, tempting us to assume conscious intelligence must be present behind such coherent responses. Proponents of artificial intelligence heavily contest Searle's conclusion through the 'Systems Reply,' arguing that while the human inside does not understand Chinese, the broader integrated system of the room, rules, and memory collectively does.

## **Source**

Formulated by philosopher John Searle in his 1980 paper *Minds, Brains, and Programs*. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on The Chinese Room Argument](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-room/).

# **October 5, 2026 — Psychology**

## **The Idea**

Cognitive dissonance is a psychological state of mental discomfort triggered when an individual holds contradictory beliefs, values, or actions simultaneously. Because the human brain craves internal psychological consistency, this tension acts as an internal drive compelling people to resolve the clash. To eliminate the discomfort, individuals typically alter their attitudes, rationalize their behavior, or reject new contradictory evidence. Consequently, people often convince themselves they believe something they previously opposed simply to justify past choices.

## **Analogy**

It is like buying an overpriced and uncomfortable pair of shoes, then convincing yourself and your friends that they look so stylish the blistering foot pain is completely worth it.

## **Nested Terms**

Effort justification refers to the psychological tendency for people to attribute exaggerated value to an outcome when they have exerted significant time, pain, or energy to achieve it.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because classic intuition assumes our beliefs dictate our actions, whereas dissonance theory proves that our actions often force our beliefs to change retroactively. Behavioral economists and psychologists debate whether dissonance reduction is an unconscious defense mechanism driven by emotional discomfort or, as Daryl Bem's self-perception theory argues, merely a cool rational deduction based on observing one's own outward behavior.

## **Source**

Formulated by American social psychologist Leon Festinger in his 1957 book *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance*. For further reading, see the [Simply Psychology entry on Cognitive Dissonance](https://www.simplypsychology.org/cognitive-dissonance.html).

# **October 6, 2026 — Philosophy**

## **The Idea**

The categorical imperative is a foundational moral principle asserting that ethical duties are absolute and binding regardless of personal desires. Immanuel Kant argued that rational beings must act only on rules they could logically will to become universal laws for everyone. If an action's underlying rule produces a logical contradiction when universalized, that action is strictly immoral. Consequently, true morality derives not from emotional sympathy or anticipated results, but from rational duty and respect for human autonomy.

## **Analogy**

It is like deciding whether to cut in a long airport queue; if everyone adopted the rule to cut whenever hurried, the concept of a queue would instantly collapse.

## **Nested Terms**

Maxim refers to the subjective, underlying personal rule or intention that motivates and guides an individual's specific action.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because people naturally evaluate right and wrong based on practical consequences and empathy, whereas Kant insists that moral duty must ignore outcomes completely. Philosophers like Benjamin Constant fiercely contested this rigidity through the 'murderer at the door' dilemma, arguing that an absolute ban on lying absurdly compels someone to reveal an innocent friend's hiding spot to an assassin.

## **Source**

Formulated by German philosopher Immanuel Kant in his 1785 work *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Kant's Moral Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-moral/).

# **October 21, 2026 — Thought Experiment**

## **The Idea**

The Experience Machine is a thought experiment challenging the claim that pleasure is the only thing that ultimately matters. Robert Nozick asks us to imagine a machine that flawlessly stimulates your brain to simulate any pleasurable life you desire. Once plugged in, you believe you are achieving great goals and enjoying deep relationships, completely unaware you are floating in a tank. Nozick argues that most people would refuse a lifetime in the machine, proving we value authentic contact with reality over mere pleasant sensations.

## **Analogy**

It is like choosing between genuinely running a marathon versus wearing a hyper-realistic virtual reality headset that tricks your brain into believing you won the race.

## **Nested Terms**

Hedonism refers to the philosophical theory that conscious pleasure is the sole intrinsic good and pain the sole intrinsic bad.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because classic hedonistic theories assume humans always seek to maximize pleasant conscious states. Critics heavily contest Nozick's conclusion, arguing that reluctance to plug in stems from status quo bias—an irrational fear of abandoning our familiar life—rather than an intrinsic desire for reality.

## **Source**

Formulated by American philosopher Robert Nozick in his 1974 book *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*. For further reading, see the [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on The Experience Machine](https://iep.utm.edu/experience-machine/).

# **October 22, 2026 — Psychology**

## **The Idea**

The Asch conformity effect demonstrates the powerful human tendency to yield to group pressure, even when the crowd is blatantly mistaken. In Solomon Asch's classic visual experiments, participants matched line lengths alongside confederates instructed to give unanimous, incorrect answers. Despite the correct answer being visually obvious, roughly 75% of participants conformed to the erroneous majority at least once. This revealed that the desire for social belonging and fear of exclusion frequently override objective sensory evidence.

## **Analogy**

It is like sitting in a meeting where every colleague praises a flawed proposal, prompting you to nod along rather than be the lone voice of dissent.

## **Nested Terms**

Normative social influence refers to the psychological pressure to conform to group expectations to gain approval and avoid rejection, even while privately disagreeing.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because individuals pride themselves on independent judgment, assuming they would never deny what their own eyes plainly see. Social psychologists debate the drivers of this conformity, finding that compliance plummets by over 80% if just one other person dissents, demonstrating that independent thinking depends heavily on social support.

## **Source**

First experimentally demonstrated by social psychologist Solomon Asch in his 1951 study *Effects of group pressure upon the modification and distortion of judgments*. For further reading, see the [Simply Psychology entry on the Asch Conformity Experiment](https://www.simplypsychology.org/asch-conformity.html).

# **October 23, 2026 — Philosophy**

## **The Idea**

The Cogito ("Cogito, ergo sum" or "I think, therefore I am") is René Descartes' foundational argument establishing the certainty of conscious existence. To discover indubitable truth, Descartes systematically doubted all sensory perceptions and external reality. He realized that while an illusion might deceive him about the physical world, the very act of doubting proves a thinking entity exists to perform that doubt. Consequently, conscious awareness serves as the bedrock of knowledge because doubting your own existence logically reaffirms it.

## **Analogy**

It is like attempting to wipe a hard drive completely clean; even if every file is deleted, the processor executing the deletion command must exist to carry it out.

## **Nested Terms**

Methodological skepticism refers to the epistemological practice of systematically doubting all beliefs to uncover an indubitable foundation of knowledge.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because we intuitively trust tangible physical objects as the most certain realities, whereas Descartes proves subjective consciousness is far more certain. Philosophers debate the premise: David Hume argued introspection reveals only passing impressions rather than a unified self, while Friedrich Nietzsche contended thinking does not prove an underlying 'I' exists.

## **Source**

Formulated by French philosopher René Descartes in his 1637 *Discourse on the Method* and 1641 *Meditations on First Philosophy*. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Descartes' Epistemology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-epistemology/).

# **October 29, 2026 — Thought Experiment**

## **The Idea**

Achilles and the Tortoise is a paradox of motion challenging continuous physical movement. In a race where a slow tortoise receives a head start, the swift Achilles can never overtake it. Before catching up, Achilles must reach the tortoise's starting point, by which time the tortoise has advanced further. When Achilles reaches that next mark, the tortoise moves again, producing an infinite series of intervals Achilles must cross before passing.

## **Analogy**

It is like walking toward an exit by continually crossing half the remaining distance; each step cuts the gap in half, creating an endless sequence of shrinking stages.

## **Nested Terms**

Infinite regress refers to a chain of reasoning where each step relies on a preceding step indefinitely without reaching a final endpoint.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because everyday experience proves fast runners effortlessly pass slower opponents, defying Zeno's logical denial of motion. Thinkers debated the puzzle for centuries until modern calculus resolved it, showing that an infinite series of shrinking fractions converges to a finite sum, enabling Achilles to pass the tortoise in finite time.

## **Source**

Formulated by pre-Socratic Greek philosopher Zeno of Elea (c. 450 BCE), recorded in Aristotle's *Physics*. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Zeno's Paradoxes](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/paradox-zeno/).

# **October 31, 2026 — Psychology**

## **The Idea**

The serial position effect is a memory phenomenon where people recall the first and last items in a sequence far better than those in the middle. Early items benefit from the primacy effect, as the brain has spare attention to rehearse and transfer them into long-term memory. Meanwhile, final items benefit from the recency effect because they remain active in short-term memory when recall begins. Consequently, middle items are forgotten fastest because they are displaced from short-term memory before reaching long-term storage.

## **Analogy**

It is like scanning a long restaurant menu or watching an awards show, where the opening and closing moments stick vividly in mind while the middle blurs together.

## **Nested Terms**

The multi-store model is a cognitive framework proposing that human memory operates through separate sensory, short-term, and long-term storage systems.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because we assume each item in a uniform list gets equal mental footing, yet a 30-second counting distraction after hearing a list completely erases the recency advantage while sparing early items. Psychologists debate whether this curve proves two separate memory stores, as single-mechanism theories like the SIMPLE model argue recall depends instead on temporal distinctiveness.

## **Source**

Formulated by psychologist Bennet Murdock in his 1962 study *The serial position effect of free recall* and expanded by Murray Glanzer and Anita Cunitz (1966). For further reading, see the [Simply Psychology entry on the Serial Position Effect](https://www.simplypsychology.org/primacy-recency.html).

# **October 31, 2026 — Philosophy**

## **The Idea**

The doctrine of double effect is an ethical principle explaining when an action that causes serious harm is morally permissible in pursuit of a good outcome. It holds that causing harm is justifiable if the act itself is good, the harm is an unintended side effect rather than a chosen means, and the good result outweighs the damage. Thus, an action's morality depends critically on whether harm is directly intended or merely foreseen.

## **Analogy**

It is like a doctor giving high-dose painkillers to relieve a terminal patient's suffering while foreseeing it may shorten life, versus giving a lethal injection to cause death.

## **Nested Terms**

Proportionality refers to the ethical requirement that an action's beneficial outcome must be weighty enough to outweigh its foreseen harmful side effects.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because two actions producing identical physical outcomes are judged differently based strictly on the actor's internal intention. Consequentialist philosophers contest this distinction through the "problem of closeness," arguing that separating an intended means from an inevitable side effect often relies on semantic framing rather than moral reality.

## **Source**

Introduced by Thomas Aquinas in his *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, Q. 64, Art. 7). For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on the Doctrine of Double Effect](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/double-effect/).

# **November 1, 2026 — Thought Experiment**

## **The Idea**

Newcomb's Paradox is a thought experiment in decision theory pitting two core principles of rational choice against each other. You face a transparent box holding \$1,000 and an opaque box holding either \$1,000,000 or nothing. Yesterday, a nearly infallible predictor foresaw your choice: if it predicted you would take only the opaque box, it put \$1,000,000 inside; if it predicted you would take both, it left the opaque box empty. You must now choose whether to take only the opaque box ("one-boxing") or take both boxes ("two-boxing").

## **Analogy**

It is like choosing whether to study for a test after an algorithm has already locked in your final grade based on a scan of your study habits.

## **Nested Terms**

Causal decision theory is the principle that rational choices should be judged strictly by the future outcomes an action directly causes, rather than what the choice signals as evidence.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because two logical rules clash: since the prediction happened yesterday, taking both boxes always yields \$1,000 more, yet one-boxers consistently walk away millionaires. Philosophers remain split between evidential decision theorists, who favor one-boxing to maximize expected payout, and causal decision theorists, who insist two-boxing is rational since present choices cannot change the past.

## **Source**

Devised by William Newcomb and published by Robert Nozick in his 1969 paper *Newcomb's Problem and Two Principles of Choice*. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Causal Decision Theory](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/decision-causal/).

# **November 2, 2026 — Psychology**

## **The Idea**

Groupthink is a social psychological phenomenon where a cohesive group's desire for harmony and unanimous agreement overrides realistic, critical evaluation of alternative options. To preserve group unity and avoid conflict, members suppress personal doubts, ignore outside warnings, and pressure dissenters to conform. As a result, collections of smart, well-intentioned individuals can make disastrous, irrational decisions they would easily avoid on their own.

## **Analogy**

It is like a group of friends hiking into an approaching storm because nobody wants to spoil the mood by suggesting they turn back, leaving everyone assuming the group agrees it is safe.

## **Nested Terms**

Concurrence-seeking refers to the strong psychological drive among group members to reach mutual agreement at the expense of critically weighing competing evidence.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because common sense assumes that strong team bonding and high morale improve collective judgment, whereas tight-knit groups often develop an "illusion of invulnerability" that blinds them to obvious risks. Later researchers like Sally Fuller, Ramon Aldag, and Robert Baron contested Irving Janis's emphasis on group cohesion, showing that directive leadership and high stress drive flawed consensus far more reliably than interpersonal closeness.

## **Source**

Formulated by social psychologist Irving Janis in his 1972 book *Victims of Groupthink*. For further reading, see the [Simply Psychology entry on Groupthink](https://www.simplypsychology.org/groupthink.html).

# **November 3, 2026 — Philosophy**

## **The Idea**

Moral luck occurs when an individual is assigned moral praise or blame for an action even though a significant aspect of its outcome or context lies completely beyond their control. While traditional ethics assumes we are morally responsible only for what we consciously intend and control, everyday judgment routinely weighs chance outcomes heavily. Consequently, people with identical intentions often face vastly different moral and legal evaluations based on sheer luck.

## **Analogy**

It is like two equally reckless drivers running a red light: one passes safely through an empty intersection and gets a traffic ticket, while the other hits a pedestrian who happened to step off the curb and is condemned as a killer.

## **Nested Terms**

The Control Principle is the moral axiom holding that individuals can be justifiably judged only for factors within their voluntary control.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because fairness seems to demand that morality be immune to chance, yet stripping away all luck—from the external results of our actions to the genetics and upbringing that shape our character—leaves almost nothing under our pure control. Philosophers remain divided: some defend the "epistemic argument" that outcomes merely reveal hidden intentions, while Thomas Nagel argued the tension exposes a fundamental paradox in human agency.

## **Source**

Introduced by philosophers Bernard Williams (1976) and Thomas Nagel (1979) in their respective essays titled *Moral Luck*. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Moral Luck](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-luck/).

# **November 4, 2026 — Thought Experiment**

## **The Idea**

Molyneux's Problem is a classic thought experiment asking how separate senses connect to form unified concepts. Suppose a person born blind can easily distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch. If their eyesight is suddenly restored and both shapes are placed on a table, could they identify which is the cube and which is the sphere by sight alone without touching them? The puzzle tests whether spatial concepts are innate across senses or learned through experience.

## **Analogy**

It is like knowing a song only by reading its sheet music, then hearing music for the first time and trying to recognize that melody by ear alone.

## **Nested Terms**

Cross-modal transfer refers to the cognitive ability to recognize a property in one sense after experiencing it only through a different sense.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because shapes seem like universal truths obvious to any sense, yet empiricists like John Locke argued a newly sighted person could not immediately map visual angles onto felt corners. Modern neuroscience tested this in 2011 (Held et al.): congenitally blind patients failed to match seen and felt shapes right after sight-restoring surgery, though their brains linked the two senses within days.

## **Source**

Posed by William Molyneux to John Locke in 1688 and published in Locke's *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1694). For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Molyneux's Problem](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/molyneux-problem/).

# **November 5, 2026 — Psychology**

## **The Idea**

Social facilitation is the psychological phenomenon where the presence of other people—either as spectators or as co-actors working alongside us—alters individual task performance. Having others nearby heightens physiological arousal, which strengthens a person's most habitual response. Consequently, an audience boosts speed and accuracy on simple, well-practiced tasks, but impairs performance on unfamiliar or complex tasks where mistakes are common.

## **Analogy**

It is like playing a piano piece you know by heart even better during a recital, while stumbling over a brand-new song the moment someone stands over your shoulder watching.

## **Nested Terms**

Dominant response refers to the reaction or behavior that is most habitual, well-learned, and likely to occur first when an individual faces a given situation.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because we often assume an audience either universally motivates people to try harder or universally causes stage fright, whereas the exact same crowd improves easy tasks and sabotages hard ones. Psychologists debate what drives this arousal: Robert Zajonc argued mere physical presence is sufficient (demonstrating the effect even in cockroaches running mazes), while Nicholas Cottrell countered that "evaluation apprehension"—the fear of being judged—is the primary human trigger.

## **Source**

First identified by Norman Triplett (1898), named by Floyd Allport (1924), and explained via drive theory by Robert Zajonc in his 1965 *Science* paper *Social Facilitation*. For further reading, see the [Simply Psychology entry on Social Facilitation](https://www.simplypsychology.org/social-facilitation.html).

# **November 6, 2026 — Philosophy**

## **The Idea**

Value pluralism is the ethical theory holding that there are multiple fundamental human values—such as liberty, equality, justice, and friendship—that cannot be reduced to a single master value like pleasure or duty. Unlike moral relativism, pluralism insists these values are objectively real, yet recognizes they frequently clash with one another in incompatible ways. Consequently, even when we make the best moral choice, we often suffer a genuine, unavoidable loss of another competing good.

## **Analogy**

It is like choosing between two completely different careers—say, becoming an investigative journalist or a concert pianist—where there is no single ruler to measure which life is objectively superior, and choosing one inevitably means giving up the rewards of the other.

## **Nested Terms**

Incommensurability refers to the state in which two competing values or goods cannot be measured or ranked along a single common scale.

## **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because classical moral systems (like utilitarianism) assume every ethical dilemma has a single mathematically optimal answer that leaves no room for rational regret once chosen. Moral monists contest value pluralism by arguing that if fundamental values truly lack a common measure, rational decision-making breaks down whenever two core duties collide.

## **Source**

Developed in modern political and moral philosophy by Isaiah Berlin (1969), Bernard Williams (1981), and W.D. Ross (1930). For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Value Pluralism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/value-pluralism/).

## **2026-11-06 — Formal Logic**

### **The Idea**

The Raven Paradox (or Hempel's Paradox) is a puzzle in formal logic asking what counts as evidence for a general rule. Suppose you test the hypothesis that *all ravens are black*—each black raven you spot naturally supports the rule. However, in classical logic, *all ravens are black* is strictly equivalent to its contrapositive: *everything that is not black is not a raven*. Because equivalent statements share the same evidence, spotting a white shoe—a non-black non-raven—logically confirms that all ravens are black.

### **Analogy**

It is like testing whether all software engineers drink coffee by spotting a tea-drinking accountant and logging that observation as proof without ever meeting an engineer.

### **Nested Terms**

*Contraposition* refers to the logical rule that a conditional statement (*if A, then B*) has the exact same truth value as its negated reverse (*if not-B, then not-A*).

### **Why It's Counterintuitive or Contested**

It is counterintuitive because common sense says you cannot study birds by inspecting shoes indoors, yet deductive logic treats both statements as identical. Philosophers debate the fix: Carl Hempel argued the paradox is a psychological illusion and that white shoes provide genuine support, whereas Bayesian theorists argue that because non-black objects vastly outnumber ravens, a white shoe adds only a near-zero fraction of confirmation.

### **Source**

Formulated by logician Carl Gustav Hempel in his 1945 paper *Studies in the Logic of Confirmation*. For further reading, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Carl Hempel](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hempel/).
